1. The goal and the big picture

Agents call customers on their personal phones and take customer calls from inside Hub, with no desk phone. Every call is matched to the right customer from the loan system the moment it rings, is recorded for audit, routed to the right team, measured, and visible to supervisors. Only the people the organization allows can use any of it, and every sensitive action is logged.

2. VoIP from scratch

A voice call over a data network has two separate halves. Signalling (SIP) sets the call up, changes it and ends it. Media (RTP, encrypted as SRTP) carries the sound in packets of 20 ms. They use different ports and often different paths: "connected but silent" almost always means signalling worked and media was blocked.

Plan 100 kbps each way per concurrent call. The PBX transcodes Opus↔G.711 (CPU sizing). Keys pressed travel as RFC 4733 DTMF events, not beeps.

WebRTC: Hub is the phone

  1. Hub asks the call service for a login signature; the service creates it on the PBX (/openapi/v1.0/sign/create). The extension password never reaches the browser.
  2. The Yeastar WebRTC SDK registers the extension with SIP over secure WebSocket (e.g. wss://pbx…:8088).
  3. Audio flows as SRTP with DTLS keys — always encrypted in WebRTC.
  4. ICE tries every network path; STUN finds the public address; a TURN relay (our coturn server) carries audio when firewalls block direct paths (home and corporate networks).

Trunks: a SIP trunk from a licensed carrier gives our public numbers (DIDs), a number of channels (simultaneous calls; running out = busy for customers) and the right to present our numbers as caller ID. Use two carriers for failover. All numbers are stored in E.164 (+254712345678).

3. Architecture and trust

Why a separate microservice

  • Bursty event load and its own failure mode: a slow call component never slows Hub, and vice versa.
  • PBX and LMS credentials live only here; stricter retention and audit for recordings and financial data.
  • All Yeastar code sits in one adapter (PBX connector) — replaceable by Asterisk/FreePBX later.

How access is enforced on the server

  1. Agent signs in to Hub. The Calls management widget is switched on for chosen groups.
  2. Hub backend issues a signed 5-minute token (person, org, calls rights).
  3. Every request and the live WebSocket carry it; the call service verifies it and checks the right for each action. Refusals are logged.

4. Yeastar: what we use, and what is open source

Correction to an assumption: only Yeastar's native mobile SDKs (linkus-android-sdk, linkus-iOS-MacOS-sdk) are published under the MIT licence. The browser SDK we need (ys-webrtc-sdk-core) is public on GitHub but states no open-source licence, and the PBX and its Open API are commercial. Written confirmation of our right to use them (and the plan that enables them) is a phase-0 task.

PBX connector behaviour: token refreshed at 80% of lifetime by one leader replica; 3 s timeout for control, 30 s for downloads; reads retried twice, "dial" never retried (query active calls instead, to avoid calling a customer twice); event socket heartbeat, reconnect with backoff 1→10 s, then resubscribe and reconcile active calls; every raw event stored before processing.

6. Caller identification with the LMS

A phone number identifies a phone, not a person. Numbers are recycled, shared, ported and spoofed. So the system identifies (suggests who is calling, instantly) and the agent verifies (asks questions) before any account detail is shown.

Inbound calls need two passed questions even from the registered number (spoofing is cheap); outbound calls we dialled need one. Three failed verifications on an account in 24 h raise a fraud alert.

  • Audit (tamper-evident): calls, verifications, number reveals, recording plays/downloads, supervision, DNC/routing/campaign changes, exports, refusals. Each row hashes the previous; daily chain check.
  • Fraud: no phone-number changes on calls (SIM-swap); random verification questions; international dialling off; PBX SIP open only to carrier IPs; night-time volume alerts; agents see only their own customers; monthly lookup review.
  • Minimisation: numbers masked by default; snapshot not full LMS record on calls; data-subject export per customer.

13. Rollout, decisions, and what was forgotten

Decisions needed

  1. Yeastar on-premises or Cloud edition
  2. Carriers, channels, second carrier
  3. Numbers per team; allowed caller IDs
  4. Written Yeastar licence for Open API + WebRTC SDK
  5. Headset model; home working (TURN, VPN)
  6. Who builds the LMS API, and when; phone data clean-up
  7. LMS write-backs (promise to pay)
  8. Recording retention; refusal policy
  9. Collection contact limits, hours, no-call days
  10. Verification questions
  11. Who may listen to recordings and supervise
  12. DPIA and DPO sign-off
  13. Hours, holidays, after-hours and 24/7 fraud line
  14. Queues, service level targets, outcome lists

What may have been forgotten

  • Swahili greetings and IVR (language choice first?)
  • Professionally recorded greetings, menus and hold messages
  • M-Pesa STK push during a call (Daraja API): a promise to pay becomes a payment immediately
  • Customer satisfaction survey after calls
  • Speech analytics on transcripts (complaint, fraud, lawyer)
  • Quality scoring of sampled calls
  • Workforce planning from call history
  • Flag for customers in hardship/bereavement to pause collections
  • Complaints register linked to calls
  • Training mode for new agents
  • Number reputation with carriers (avoid "likely scam" labels)
  • Mobile app for agents (Linkus SDKs, MIT)
